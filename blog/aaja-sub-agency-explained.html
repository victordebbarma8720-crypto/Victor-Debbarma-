Aaja Sub-Agency Explained: How to Expand Your Team

Once an Aaja Agency grows past a certain point, supporting every host personally becomes difficult. This is where the idea of a sub-agency comes in — a way to extend your reach without stretching yourself too thin. This guide explains what a sub-agency actually is, when it makes sense, and how to set one up without losing the quality that made your agency successful in the first place.

What Is a Sub-Agency?

A sub-agency is essentially a smaller agency operating underneath a larger parent agency. Instead of you personally managing every single host, a trusted sub-agency leader takes on a group of hosts, handling their onboarding, day-to-day support, and check-ins, while you oversee the wider structure. It's a way of adding a management layer so your agency can grow beyond what one person can realistically support.

If you're still building your first team, it's worth reading our guide on joining Aaja Agency before thinking about sub-agencies — this structure generally makes sense only once your core agency is already running smoothly.

It helps to think of a sub-agency less like a separate business and more like a branch of your existing one. The people, standards, and communication style should feel consistent with what you've already built, even though a different person is handling the day-to-day relationship with a particular group of hosts. When this consistency is missing, hosts under a sub-agency can end up with a noticeably different experience than hosts under the parent agency, which creates confusion and, over time, frustration.

Why Agencies Choose to Expand This Way

As your host team grows, a few practical problems tend to show up: response times to hosts slow down, onboarding quality can slip because you're stretched across too many people, and it becomes harder to notice which hosts are quietly losing motivation. A sub-agency structure addresses this by distributing support work across more people who each manage a smaller, more manageable group.

  • Faster response times for hosts, since sub-agency leaders handle smaller groups directly.
  • More personalised onboarding, since each new host gets closer attention.
  • Room to keep growing your total team size without sacrificing support quality.
  • An opportunity for trusted, experienced hosts to take on more responsibility and earn in a new way.

There's also a simpler, more human reason agencies expand this way: burnout. Managing dozens of hosts personally, day after day, is genuinely tiring, and it's easy to start giving each host less attention without meaning to. A layered structure spreads that load across more people, which often means every host — no matter which layer they're in — gets a more attentive, sustainable level of support than one overstretched person could provide alone.

How Sub-Agencies Work in Practice

In a typical structure, the parent agency remains responsible for the overall relationship and structure, while the sub-agency leader focuses on the day-to-day work of supporting a specific group of hosts — answering questions, following up on tasks, and keeping communication active. The parent agency usually stays available for bigger-picture guidance and support, especially early in the sub-agency's development.

This layered approach mirrors how the broader Aaja Agency system is built, where support and responsibility flow through a chain rather than resting entirely on one person.

Setting Up a Sub-Agency

If you're ready to expand this way, a few steps help make the transition smooth:

  • Choose carefully: Pick someone who already understands the platform well and has shown reliability, ideally an experienced host from your own team.
  • Set clear expectations: Be specific about what the sub-agency leader is responsible for and how often you expect updates from them.
  • Provide the same onboarding quality: Share the same materials and guidance you used to build your own agency, such as our host tips for beginners, so standards stay consistent.
  • Confirm the commission and structure details: Reach out to our support team to confirm how current arrangements work between agency layers before finalising anything.

You can also review our Aaja Agency Commission Structure guide for general context on how commission flows through the system, though exact figures should always be confirmed directly with support.

Give the sub-agency leader a short trial period before fully handing off a group of hosts. Start with a smaller number of hosts under their care, observe how they handle onboarding and everyday questions, and expand their responsibilities gradually as they demonstrate they can maintain the standard you've set. This protects both your hosts and your sub-agency leader from being overwhelmed too quickly.

Managing Multiple Layers Without Losing Control

Growth through sub-agencies only works if quality stays consistent at every layer — a bigger structure with weaker support is not actually progress.

Stay involved even after handing off day-to-day management. Regular check-ins with your sub-agency leaders — not just their hosts — help you catch issues early. Treat the relationship as a partnership rather than a one-time handoff, and keep communication open in both directions so problems surface before they grow.

Risks and Responsibilities to Keep in Mind

Expanding through sub-agencies adds complexity, and it's worth going in with clear eyes. If a sub-agency leader isn't performing well, it reflects on your whole structure, so choose people you trust and stay engaged rather than stepping back completely. It also helps to build this gradually — start with one sub-agency, see how it runs, and only add more once you're confident in the process. If you have questions about structuring this responsibly, our team is available through the contact page or WhatsApp.

It's also worth thinking honestly about whether expansion is the right move for you personally, not just for your team's size. Some agency owners genuinely enjoy the added layer of coordination that comes with managing sub-agencies, while others find they prefer staying closely involved with a smaller group of hosts directly. Neither approach is wrong — a smaller, tightly run agency can be just as successful as a larger, layered one, as long as it stays consistent and well-supported. Growth for its own sake rarely pays off if it comes at the cost of the quality that made your agency work in the first place.

If you do decide to expand, document what you've learned as you go. Simple notes on what worked during onboarding, common questions hosts ask, and how you've handled difficult situations become genuinely useful material to hand off to a sub-agency leader, saving them from repeating the same trial and error you already went through.
